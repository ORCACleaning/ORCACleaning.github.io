MONEY · TAX AND REPORTING

Platforms now report your short-stay income to the ATO

Last reviewed: 17 November 2026

Since July 2023, booking platforms have given the ATO regular information about short-stay transactions. Here is what owners must declare, what may be deductible, what changed for holiday homes in 2026, and why records matter.

9 min read

What changed
Covered booking platforms report short-stay transactions to the ATO twice a year, and in May 2026 the ATO issued new guidance on rental deductions and holiday homes.
Who it affects
Property owners and tenant-operators earning short-stay accommodation income, and co-hosts or property managers receiving fees for their services. Their reporting and tax treatment may differ.
What to do
Reconcile gross rental income, calculate allowable deductions carefully, keep adequate records and seek registered tax advice when needed.

The quiet part of running a short stay is the paperwork. A host who lists a Scarborough apartment on two platforms, takes a few direct bookings and keeps the odd forfeited deposit is now covered by a more systematic reporting framework, while older ATO data-matching programs show that accommodation income has been under scrutiny for years. The money still arrives the same way. What has changed is how much the ATO already knows. For a Perth operator heading into peak season, the sensible response is not worry: it is good records and an honest return.

The platforms report twice a year

The mechanism is the Sharing Economy Reporting Regime. Since 1 July 2023, operators of covered booking platforms have had to report short-term accommodation transactions to the ATO. Transactions from 1 July to 31 December are reported by 31 January, and transactions from 1 January to 30 June by 31 July.

That sits on top of earlier work. One ATO data-matching program estimated it would collect records about up to 190,000 individuals from accommodation platforms for the 2016–17 to 2019–20 years, including fields such as date of birth and nights booked. The ATO can use this information for compliance and data matching, but platform income may not automatically appear in a taxpayer's return. Owners remain responsible for reporting their income accurately.

Owners and managers report different income. An owner generally reports gross rental income, before platform commissions and property-management fees are deducted. A manager or co-host generally accounts for their own service income under the rules that apply to their business. Money collected for an owner is not automatically the manager's income; the correct treatment depends on the arrangement and whose income the payment is.

Declare everything, not just the rent

Leaving income out is on the ATO's list of common mistakes. You must declare all the income you receive from renting out a property, and the ATO's list expressly includes short-term rentals and renting through a sharing platform. It does not stop at the nightly tariff. Bond or deposit money you keep in place of rent or because of damage counts as income, and so can insurance payouts for lost rent. Where a property is co-owned, rental income is generally divided according to the owners' legal interests, subject to limited exceptions. How deductible expenses are shared also depends on the ownership interests and the nature of the costs.

Deductions: only the income-earning share

The ATO requires expenses to be apportioned on a fair and reasonable basis when a property, or part of it, is used for more than one purpose or rented for only part of the year. For an ordinary rental property, periods actually rented and periods genuinely available on commercial terms can both affect the split. A property is not genuinely available if, for example, it is advertised well above a reasonable market rate. Days you, family or friends use the property are not deductible.

A newer test for holiday homes. On 20 May 2026 the ATO published new guidance: Taxation Ruling TR 2026/1 on rental property income and deductions, and two practical compliance guidelines covering expense apportionment (PCG 2026/2) and holiday homes that are also rented out (PCG 2026/3). Where a property is used or held for holidays or recreation, deductions for ownership and use expenses may be denied unless an exception applies. A key exception covers holiday homes used or held mainly to produce assessable rental income throughout the income year. The ATO looks at the property's actual use, its availability during popular holiday periods and other circumstances, not simply the share of nights advertised. Where the exception does not apply, deductions for ownership and use expenses, including interest, council and water rates, body corporate fees, capital works and decline in value, may be denied. Directly income-related costs, such as advertising, cleaning after a guest stay, and booking fees and commissions, may remain deductible. The ruling sets out the ATO's view of the law, which can apply to earlier income years. However, the ATO has said it will not devote compliance resources to reviewing whether section 26-50 applies to relevant holiday-home expenses incurred before 1 July 2026. If your short stay doubles as a family holiday place, check this guidance before you calculate your claim.

Not every expense is deductible straight away. Eligible running costs and repairs relating to rental use may be deductible in the income year they are incurred. Repairs to defects that existed when you bought the property, improvements and replacement assets can be treated differently. New depreciating assets costing more than A$300, such as a new oven or dishwasher, are generally claimed over their effective life, and eligible capital works, such as a new kitchen, are deducted over time. Special restrictions can apply to previously used depreciating assets in residential rentals. Interest needs care too: money redrawn from the loan for a private purpose, a holiday or a car, is not deductible even though the loan is secured against the rental.

GST: why the type of accommodation matters

The supply of ordinary residential premises by way of rent is generally input taxed. That means you do not charge GST on that accommodation income and generally cannot claim GST credits for costs relating to it. Input-taxed rent is left out when working out GST turnover for the registration threshold, but an operator with other business activities may still need to register. Accommodation in commercial residential premises, such as hotels, motels and some serviced-apartment operations, can be treated differently. Running several short stays or adding services does not automatically put you in that category; if your arrangement is unclear, get advice from a registered tax professional who knows GST.

Capital gains and the family home

The surprise can come years later, on sale. Renting out part of your main residence to earn assessable income can reduce the capital gains tax main-residence exemption when you eventually sell. The result depends on the circumstances, including how much of the home was used and for how long. In some circumstances the "home first used to produce income" rule treats the property as acquired at its market value when it first started producing income. That rule has eligibility conditions and exceptions, so do not assume it applies to every home or every short-stay arrangement. Keep a record of when income-producing use began and get advice on valuation and CGT treatment.

Where hosts get caught

In May 2024 the ATO said its data showed nine in ten rental-property owners were getting their income tax returns wrong. That figure covered rental-property owners generally, not specifically WA short-stay operators, and it is not a fresh 2026 error rate. The ATO continues to emphasise complete income reporting, correct classification of expenses and records that support every claim. The principles are manageable, but applying them correctly can be complicated. Good records make that work easier before a return is lodged.

"Our data shows 9 out of 10 rental property owners are getting their income tax returns wrong."ATO, May 2024

A — Records to keep from today

Keep separate records for each property throughout ownership. Keep purchase, improvement and sale records for at least five years after you sell, and keep the records behind each tax return for the required period.

  • Every platform payout statement
  • Records of direct bookings and money received
  • Bond or deposit amounts you kept, and why
  • Any insurance payout for lost rent
  • Loan and interest statements
  • Council rates, water and strata levy notices
  • Invoices for repairs, cleaning, servicing and consumables
  • A simple log of nights rented, nights genuinely available, and any private use
  • Floor-area notes if you rent only part of the home
  • Purchase and sale contracts and improvement receipts (for capital gains tax)

B — Common mistakes the ATO looks for

From the ATO's own statements:

  • Leaving income out, including short-term rent, kept bond money and insurance payouts.
  • Claiming interest on a loan, or part of a loan, used for private spending.
  • Treating a capital improvement (a new kitchen) as an immediate repair.
  • Claims inflated to offset higher rental income.
  • Claiming for periods the owner, family or friends used the property.
  • Records that cannot support the claim.

C — Worked example: claiming part-home expenses (illustrative figures only)

A host rents out one bedroom in their home to paying guests for 120 nights during the income year. The bedroom is 20 per cent of the home's floor area. Guests can also use shared living areas, which are another 20 per cent. For illustration, the host attributes half the shared space to guest use. The rental-use area is therefore 30 per cent: 20 per cent exclusive bedroom space plus 10 per cent shared space. If eligible annual expenses total A$20,000, an illustrative apportionment is: A$20,000 × 30% × (120 ÷ 365) = A$1,973. This assumes the room was actually rented for those 120 nights and that the area and time split is reasonable. Expenses incurred only for the rental (such as a platform fee) may be treated separately. Your actual figures depend on your property, your expenses and how guests use it.

What this means for WA hosts

Bottom line: Covered platforms report short-stay transactions to the ATO. Owners should reconcile their gross rental income; managers and co-hosts should correctly account for their own service income.

Do this:

  1. Reconcile gross platform earnings, including host and cleaning fees, with booking statements and other rental receipts.
  2. Include direct bookings, kept deposits and relevant insurance proceeds.
  3. Separate private and rental use, and apply the ATO's apportionment rules, including the May 2026 holiday-home guidance if it applies to you.
  4. Identify eligible repairs, initial repairs, depreciating assets and capital improvements, because each is treated differently.
  5. Consider a registered tax agent, particularly for mixed private use, GST classification, CGT or complicated ownership.
